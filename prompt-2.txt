Your job is to research the brand, understand its products and visual identity, come up with a creative direction, and actually build and render the final video using the tools available in this environment.

Don't just give me a storyboard or write a script. I want you to produce the actual video.

1. First, understand the brand

Visit https://chilsandco.com/ and explore the website carefully.

Understand:

- What the brand sells.
- Who its target customers are.
- What makes its products different.
- Its visual identity, including colors, typography, imagery, and overall aesthetic.
- How the brand communicates with customers.
- What emotions the brand wants to evoke.
- Which products or collections deserve the most attention in a promotional video.

Look at the actual product images, website design, logos, and other available brand assets.

Use the existing website as your primary reference. Don't invent product features, prices, offers, ingredients, or brand claims.

If you cannot access the website from your current environment, tell me and ask me for the necessary assets instead of pretending you've researched it.

2. Come up with a genuinely creative concept

Once you understand the brand, develop a creative concept for a short promotional video.

I want you to think like a creative director, not just an AI coding assistant.

Don't immediately jump into a generic sequence of product shots, floating text, gradients, and transitions.

Find an interesting way to present the brand. Think about how the products can be revealed, how the visuals can tell a story, and how the video can capture attention within the first few seconds.

The creative direction should feel appropriate for Chils & Co., not like a template that could be used for any business.

You can explore cinematic product reveals, editorial typography, sophisticated transitions, layered compositions, 3D-inspired movement, dynamic product layouts, or other techniques that suit the brand.

However, don't use effects simply because they look impressive. Every visual decision should support the product and the overall creative concept.

Before implementing the video, briefly explain the concept you've chosen and why it fits the brand. Then move directly into production.

3. Make it look like professional motion design

I want the final result to feel polished enough that the brand could genuinely use it on Instagram Reels, YouTube Shorts, its website, or a paid advertising campaign.

Pay attention to:

- Composition and visual hierarchy.
- Typography and text placement.
- Smooth, natural animation.
- Sophisticated transitions between scenes.
- Depth, lighting, shadows, and perspective where appropriate.
- Product image quality and positioning.
- Visual pacing and rhythm.
- Color consistency with the brand.
- Sound design and music synchronization.

Avoid the common AI video aesthetic of centered text on a gradient background with random floating shapes.

Use the real product photography and brand assets wherever possible. Don't replace actual products with inaccurate AI-generated versions.

If the website has high-quality product photography, use it creatively. The motion design should enhance the original product imagery rather than obscure it.

4. Build and render the video programmatically

Use the available tools to create the video rather than merely describing how someone could make it.

Inspect the existing development environment before installing anything.

Where appropriate, use:

- Node.js for orchestration.
- HTML, CSS, JavaScript, SVG, or Canvas for scene creation.
- Playwright to render animation frames in a browser.
- FFmpeg to combine frames, encode the final MP4, and process audio.

You can use other tools if they are better suited to a particular task.

Implement smooth, deterministic animation. Use proper easing, spring-based motion where appropriate, and carefully controlled timing.

Make sure the scenes are rendered at consistent timestamps and the final video has no missing frames, broken assets, unintended blank screens, or incomplete transitions.

If you need additional assets, look for suitable brand-owned assets first. Don't download random images and pass them off as Chils & Co. products.

5. Pay special attention to the first three seconds

The opening is extremely important.

Create a visual hook that immediately captures attention and makes the viewer want to continue watching.

Don't start with a long logo animation or a generic title card unless the creative concept genuinely calls for it.

The opening should introduce the brand or product in an interesting, visually compelling way.

Every subsequent scene should feel like a deliberate continuation of the same creative idea.

6. Make the sound design feel intentional

If appropriate for the brand and concept, incorporate background music, subtle sound effects, and audio accents.

Synchronize important product reveals, transitions, and visual movements with the audio.

Don't add random sound effects just to make the video feel more elaborate.

Use audio assets that are available and properly licensed for the intended use. If suitable music isn't available, create a version that can accept a soundtrack later rather than using an unlicensed commercial track.

Keep the sound design consistent with the brand's personality.

7. Review your own work and improve it

Don't consider the video finished just because the rendering process succeeds.

Inspect the actual rendered output.

Extract representative frames and examine the video for:

- Incorrect product imagery.
- Poor composition.
- Text clipping or readability issues.
- Inconsistent typography.
- Awkward transitions.
- Excessive empty space.
- Animation that feels mechanical.
- Inconsistent branding.
- Timing problems.
- Visual elements that distract from the products.

Use programmatic validation and vision-based review where available.

Fix the issues you identify and render the video again.

Don't claim that you've reviewed the video unless you've actually inspected the rendered output.

8. Deliver an actual video file

My primary requirement is the finished promotional video, not a complicated application or a large collection of source files.

Create a high-quality MP4 suitable for social media.

Unless the brand or creative direction suggests otherwise, start with a vertical 9:16 format at 1080 × 1920 resolution.

Aim for approximately 20–30 seconds, with the final duration determined by the strength of the concept.

Also keep the source project organized so I can modify the video later.

Save the final output in an obvious location, such as:

output/chils-and-co-promo.mp4

Provide a short README explaining how to render the video again and how to change the product assets, text, and timing.

If the first concept would work better in a different format, explain why before committing to that format.

Make sensible creative and technical decisions without asking me to approve every small detail.

However, don't invent information about the brand or pretend a feature works when it doesn't.

If a dependency is missing, investigate and install it if possible.

If you cannot generate a particular effect with the available tools, find another way to achieve a similar result.

Don't stop after producing the first rough version. Review it and improve it.
